For the 2026 California QSO Party, our friends Rob K6KM and Dean N6DE invited us to join them for a quick immersion into the world of contesting. They planned to activate Lassen County and Shasta County. Usually there are few stations on the air from these counties, so one station makes a big difference!

This was a wonderful experience for the kids and me. I couldn’t help noticing some similarities with SOTA: setting up in the middle of nowhere, preferably with good takeoff angles. And also the differences: everything is optimized for propagation, and everyone is thoroughly prepared for everything, with a healthy margin. It was their contest, and they did all the hard work; we only joined briefly. We helped make a few contacts from the portable contest stations, and yes, it feels different!

This whole corner of California is special. It’s where the Cascades end and the Sierra Nevada begins, with Lassen Peak in the middle: volcanoes, lava flows and cinder cones, pine forests, and high, dry plateaus stretching east toward Nevada. Lots of land, very few people. Great for radio!

Getting there

West Prospect Peak is in Lassen National Forest, just outside the northern edge of Lassen Volcanic National Park. A long Forest Service road (32N12) goes all the way to the top. It’s all dirt, but fine for any car. Our track starts at the end of the pavement: 10.2 miles and about 3,200′ of climbing to the summit, about 40 minutes each way.

  • When: summer and fall, once the snow is gone. The lookout is staffed during fire season.
  • Time: the drive is the long part. Leave time to chat at the top!
  • Radio: public land, no permission needed. The lookout is a workplace, though: say hi before setting up near the tower. Expect RFI on VHF, with the radio equipment on top; HF worked fine.

Trailhead

On Sunday the contest was wrapping up, and it was time to head home (Monday is a school day). We started later, with a plan: join Dean and Rob on West Prospect Peak, and do an activation on the way. The road up is long and dusty, but decent even for a city sedan. However, by the time we made it to the top, they had already left.

Summit

Instead we found a fire lookout tower, still in use! The Civilian Conservation Corps built it in 1935, to replace the old lookout on neighboring Prospect Peak, about 2 miles away inside the national park (SummitPost). During fire season someone lives up here, watching the forest for smoke all day. And what a view to watch: Lassen Peak is right there in the distance, about 10 miles to the southwest.

The lookout on duty was amazing. She also had a visitor: a fellow lookout from another tower nearby, with her dog, Lucky Girl.

The kids had a blast playing with Lucky Girl and convincing her to sing. I kid you not, she sang almost like the borzoi in Pink Floyd’s Mademoiselle Nobs!

We started chatting, and it quickly became obvious this was not an ordinary chat. We spent hours talking about everything: the scientific (astronomy and particle physics), the deep (cosmology and metaphysics), and the mundane (California’s education system). Somewhere in between, the bristlecone pines of the White Mountains, some of them more than 4,000 years old, and camping up there under some of the darkest skies around. Now that’s a trip for another time! Our host also pointed out the radio telescope, the Allen Telescope Array at Hat Creek, about 16 miles to the north-northwest.

About an hour before the new UTC day, we went outside, set up our antennas, and went on the air at 3:53 PM, just after CQP ended. 40m CW, with the three of us taking turns: 7 contacts for me, including a summit-to-summit with Termination Point (W7W/LC-084) in Washington and two on 15m, then 8 for KO6MFP and 5 for KO6MFO. The skimmers of the Reverse Beacon Network heard us 13 times. Off the air at 4:44 PM, and back inside.

A reminder: points are not everything. On this one, SOTA took second place, and that was fine.

And back

Then back inside for another round of chatting. We could have gone on for a long time, but it was getting late, and our host warned us we’d find everything closed on the way home. So she packed a dinner for us! She was right: everything was closed by the time we reached the first town.

We’ve met many interesting people on our trips, but this was something else: a rare gift.

Then the long drive back home. The next morning, kids in school, and back to work.
