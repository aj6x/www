Mount Rose is the highest summit in the Carson Range, 10,776′, above Incline Village and Lake Tahoe’s north shore: the lake on one side, Reno and the Truckee Meadows on the other. The trail up is one of the most popular hikes around, and on top is a long, rocky ridge with room for several activators at once. That’s how I met two of them, on the same morning.

My first serious SOTA trip to Tahoe was in August 2022, four months after a first try on Tamarack Peak next door: five summits in four days. Mount Rose was calling, the highest summit in the cluster above Incline Village. There are easier ways to get 10 points around here, but that day it had to be Rose. And few people activated it back then: only a handful of activations a year.

Getting there

The trail starts at the Mount Rose Summit Trailhead, on the pass of the Mount Rose Highway (NV-431), the same trailhead as for Tamarack Peak, Mount Houghton and Slide Mountain, and even the same first stretch of trail as Tamarack and Houghton. Slide goes the other way. About 5 miles to the top and about 2,000′ of climbing, most of it in the last mile and a half. The summit is in the Mount Rose Wilderness, in the Humboldt-Toiyabe National Forest.

  • When: we went in August and September, with the trail clear and the meadows still green.
  • Water: none at the trailhead. Galena Creek runs along the middle of the hike, but I bring my own.
  • Time: about three hours up for us, with photo stops, and about as long down.
  • Sun: some shade under the pines on the way to the waterfall, then less and less. The last climb and the top are in full sun.
  • Crowds: lots of people, especially on the way to the waterfall. It’s surprising how many go all the way up: not a trivial hike.
  • Dogs: lots of them.
  • Wilderness: no permit needed for a day hike; groups of up to 15, and no bikes (Forest Service).
  • Toilets: at the trailhead.

Trailhead

A big paved lot on the pass, with restrooms. Same sign as for Tamarack: worth a read on the way in.

The way up

The trail starts out climbing a bit, about 300′ in the first mile, then runs nearly flat, even slightly downhill, for a long stretch to Galena Falls, 2.5 miles in. Many people turn around here: a good hike on its own.

Past the falls the trail goes through the meadows of upper Galena Creek, crossing the creek a few times, among willows, corn lilies and wildflowers. The Forest Service keeps a list of what blooms here, mid-June to mid-August: lupines, paintbrush, columbines, elephant’s heads and many more. Then it starts rising again, to the Mount Rose Wilderness sign, 3.8 miles in, at about 9,750′.

From here the trail climbs steeply all the way up: 1,000′ in about a mile and a quarter, in switchbacks up the open slope to the summit ridge.

Summit

The top is a long, rocky ridge with rock wind breaks and a U.S. Coast and Geodetic Survey reference mark. Lake Tahoe to the south, Reno and the Truckee Meadows to the east, and on a clear day the reservoirs north of Truckee.

The activation zone is long. All three times I set up at the same spot, at the far end of the ridge, about 150 yards past the high point, by a low stone wind break.

When I got to the top, I saw two masts with antennas, one on each side of the summit. What were the chances? Three activators on the same summit, the same morning.

I walked over to the first one, said hi, and we started talking. We were clearly enjoying it, but we both had activations to do. That’s how I met Derek, W7DLZ. We went on to do many activations together, and soon the kids started joining us.

Then I walked over to the other side, where David, K7DMJ, was activating too, and was happy to stop and chat. Another great first meeting: the next day we activated Snow Valley Peak together. Soon after he went back to Utah. No more activations together since, but some fascinating conversations about minerals, X-rays and neutrons.

I keep saying that at first sight SOTA is already more than the sum of its parts, hiking plus radio. But the best part is meeting and getting to know all these fascinating people, some of them already in my log many times over. Everyone is busy balancing family, work, hiking, radio and other hobbies, yet always open to meeting someone new. A friend (radio yes, SOTA no) once told me: “Some of my friends do SOTA. They are in a cult!” I can agree with that. It has all the good parts of a cult, and none of the bad ones.

Anyway, back to the activation. Derek had the east side and David the west, but the activation zone is long, so I walked on to the far end and set up by the wind break. That summer I was experimenting with quarter-wave verticals for 20m and up, and carried a 40m EFHW for 40m. So I laid out my 12 radials, raised the vertical and the EFHW, and got on the air at 11:50 AM.

It was a busy S2S day, and a nice spot, so I kept going for a long time: 44 contacts, 23 of them S2S, until 3:06 PM. 30 on 20m, 12 on 40m and 2 on 15m, all SSB. Arizona was busy: eight of the S2S were Arizona 10-pointers. The noise was noticeably higher than on Tamarack, but still great, unlike on Slide Mountain nearby.

About an hour in, Derek and David stopped by: they had finished and were heading down, and wanted to say bye. And all the time, curious hikers stopped by to find out what I was doing.

And back

The same way down, the same trail.

The next two years I came back with Derek to the same spot, with just the 40m EFHW. In 2025 and 2026 we started traveling much farther for new summits, so Mount Rose is still waiting for our fourth joint activation.
