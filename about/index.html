Who we are

AJ6X. I’m Gabriel. I grew up in the 1980s behind the Iron Curtain, where my window to the world was my parents’ ancient shortwave radio, with vacuum tubes. They let the 10-year-old me listen too, after warning me to keep it absolutely secret. A few years later I managed to get the radio into my room, and browsed the short waves until the early hours: the 31 m band at night, the 19 m band in the morning, through fading, drift and jamming, with a stealth wire antenna along the outside wall of our house. Radio Free Europe was a shining light in a dark period.

Decades later, an IEEE Spectrum article about building a time machine for radio brought it all back, and a radio project at work gave me the extra push to get my ham license. Then I got busy discovering everything you can do when you can also transmit: DXing, ARES/RACES, and above all Summits On The Air.

SOTA turned out to be much more than the sum of its parts, hiking plus radio. It takes us to places we’d never have seen otherwise, and it keeps bringing very interesting people into our lives: chasers who become familiar voices, activators on the next summit, people met on the trail. Some of them have become good friends. No ambitious goals for this site: I write things down when I solve a problem that isn’t documented anywhere else.

KO6MFO. Alex is in elementary school. We’ve been hiking and activating together for five years now, and Alex often jumped in to help with the SSB contacts, as third-party traffic under my callsign. This year Alex got a license too, and can finally activate as KO6MFO. Most summits need HF, though, and on HF a Technician license means mostly CW, so Alex had to learn CW fast. The activator points are adding up quickly.

KO6MFP. Sophie is in middle school, and has been on the same five years of summits, often taking the microphone for SSB contacts long before having a callsign. This year came the license, and with it activations as KO6MFP. Same story with HF: a Technician license meant learning CW, and fast. Sophie did, and is quickly collecting activator points too.

The AJ6X RF scale

Some summits are crowded with transmitters: public safety, paging, broadcast, data links. Close up, they can overload a radio’s receiver until it hears nothing else. So each summit’s fact sheet names the cheapest radio that should still work there, on HF and on 2 m, on a ladder of four rungs:

Rung 2 m HF What it’s like
1 Baofeng, about $25 uSDX or QMX, about $150 Anything works, even the one from the glovebox.
2 Kenwood TH-D75A, about $650 Elecraft KX2, about $1,100 Only the best receiver copes.
3 TH-D75A + TinyFilter, about $700 KX2 + K9DP filter, about $1,140 The best receiver behind a filter, and you’re still on the air.
4 Go home Go home Nothing works. Eat your sandwich, take a photo, enjoy the view.

The rung comes from the transmitters around the summit in the FCC’s licence data, and how strong a signal each radio can take. When a cheap radio behind a filter would do, the fact sheet says so too, and pulsing means a pager nearby: expect the receiver to blank every few seconds.

For now, take it as an educated guess, not a measurement. It’s built from public records, published lab numbers and what I’ve heard on a handful of summits, and checked against one set of field measurements, made by Alice KO6KZO and Tony KM6AM on San Bruno Mountain. A more systematic approach is in the works, with the full story in What to bring to a noisy summit.

Maps

Every map on this site is drawn here, by our own scripts, from free and open data. There are no map tiles or map services behind the pages: the maps are plain vector images, made once for each article and served from this site.

  • Summit maps: elevation, contours and relief shading from the USGS 3D Elevation Program; water, roads and trails from OpenStreetMap, together with the U.S. Forest Service’s roads and the USGS National Digital Trails; public land from the BLM’s Surface Management Agency layer and the USGS Protected Areas Database (PAD-US); federal access easements from the MAPLand Act data; aerial photos from the USDA’s National Agriculture Imagery Program (NAIP), through the USGS National Map; magnetic declination from the World Magnetic Model; activation zones from activation.zone (N6ARA).
  • Where a summit is: counties from the U.S. Census Bureau (TIGER); land and wilderness from PAD-US and the BLM; elsewhere, or as a fallback, from Peakbagger.com, which also gives the prominence and isolation.
  • Cell service: the carriers’ own coverage maps (AT&T, T-Mobile, Verizon; 4G LTE, outdoors), as filed with the FCC for its National Broadband Map, sampled along each route.
  • RF nearby: licensed transmitters from the FCC (land mobile, paging, cellular, broadcast, antenna structures) and NOAA Weather Radio; masts and power lines from OpenStreetMap; terrain from the USGS 3D Elevation Program; receivers from the ARRL lab’s published measurements. The RF scale is our own rough estimate.
  • QSO maps: the earth from NASA’s Blue Marble, borders from Natural Earth, summits from the SOTA summits list, spots and skimmers’ grid squares from the Reverse Beacon Network. The only data that isn’t open: where each station is, looked up on QRZ.com and rounded to the middle of its grid square (about 5 by 4 km), so no map points at anyone’s home.

Each map credits its sources in its caption. The maps are shared under the same licence as everything else on this site, CC BY-NC-ND 4.0. In plain words: you’re welcome to copy, print and pass them on, as long as you credit AJ6X, don’t sell them or use them commercially, and share them as they are, not changed or made into something new. For anything beyond that, just ask. The data they are made from keeps its own licence (OpenStreetMap’s is the ODbL), so you can always go to the sources and make your own.
